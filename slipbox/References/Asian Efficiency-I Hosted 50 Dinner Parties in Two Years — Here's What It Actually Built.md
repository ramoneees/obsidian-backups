# I Hosted 50 Dinner Parties in Two Years — Here's What It Actually Built

**Source:** Asian Efficiency (Thanh Pham)
**Date:** 2026-07-25
**URL:** https://www.asianefficiency.com/productivity/fifty-dinner-parties-in-two-years/

## TL;DR

Thanh moved to Austin in 2014 with no local network. Built one by hosting **50 dinner parties over two years** — small, intentional, no agenda. The compounding result was unexpected: business partnerships, collaborations, a couple who met at one of his dinners now engaged. The piece argues that **quality relationships are the highest-ROI long-term investment**, validated by Harvard's 80-year study and Pillemer's *30 Lessons for Living* (1,000+ end-of-life interviews). The behavioral gap is the real problem: most people agree intellectually but don't actually invest.

## The starting point: knowing *of* vs. knowing

Thanh had a business, a network, had been to events, joined groups. But one day realized he knew almost everyone only at the surface — first names, job titles, casual run-ins. **"I knew *of* people. I didn't actually know them."**

## The book that triggered it

[*30 Lessons for Living* by Karl Pillemer](https://www.amazon.com/30-Lessons-Living-Advice-Americans/) — interviews with 1,000+ elderly Americans about what they'd pass on. The answer across every background: **quality relationships**. Not career, money, or status.

> Most people invest in work and growth but not in the people around them. Not really.

## Starting small

Decision wasn't a big ambitious project. It was: **host dinner parties**. Invite people he already knew but didn't truly know. No agenda. No networking objective. Just dinner and conversation with a small group.

- **First dinner:** 7 people. He almost talked himself out of hosting it. Everyone showed up. Conversation wasn't awkward. People opened up in ways they don't at events or happy hours.
- **Cadence:** monthly. Then regular. Then fifty over two years.

## What compounded

Dinners stayed small (8–10 people, sometimes slightly larger). All followed the same basic structure: invite people he genuinely wanted to know, create a relaxed environment, let conversation go where it wanted.

**Unexpected outcomes:**
- Business partnerships formed at the table.
- People who met at one dinner collaborated on projects he didn't know about until months later.
- A woman from one event co-founded something helping hundreds of people — she thanked him later.
- Two people he introduced to play pickleball (both new to Austin) ended up engaged; he was asked to the wedding.

> "None of that was the plan. The only intention was: host a dinner. See who shows up. Be curious about people."

## The principle

> "When you consistently put good out into the world without expecting anything in return, good comes back. You can't time it. You can't schedule it. You can't put it on a quarterly plan. But it shows up."

Opportunities surface through people you connected with years ago. Problems get solved because someone in your network knows the right person. Moments of warmth show up when you need them. **It's the long-run math of investing in other people. It compounds.**

## The broader lesson

The Harvard 80-year study and *30 Lessons for Living* converge on the same conclusion: **quality of close relationships** determines how life goes, more than almost anything else.

We know this. Intellectually we agree. **The gap is behavior.** Most of us invest in work and treat relationships as something that fits in the schedule when there's room.

The specific action doesn't have to be dinner parties. But it has to be:
- Intentional.
- Create real face time with people you actually care about.
- Slow you down enough to actually get to know someone.

> "Start small. Invite five people. See what happens. The ROI is harder to measure than most things. But looking back ten years, it's the one I'm most grateful I made."
