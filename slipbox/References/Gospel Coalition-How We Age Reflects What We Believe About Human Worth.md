# How We Age Reflects What We Believe About Human Worth

**Source**: The Gospel Coalition (book review)
**Reviewer**: Amy Mantravadi
**Book reviewed**: _Dust to Dust: Aging Wisely in an Anti-Aging World_ by Jen Wilkin (Crossway, 208 pp)
**Date**: 2026-07-21
**URL**: https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/reviews/dust-to-dust-aging/

## TL;DR

A review of Jen Wilkin's _Dust to Dust_ — argues that how we think about aging is shaped by what we believe about the value of human life at every stage. Wilkin reframes aging as a chiasm: progressive development to middle age, then gradual decline to final breath — but with growing wisdom. The book's core move is calling older adults "people to love, not problems to solve."

## Key Points

### Why a book on aging now
Christians strive to "value all of human life, from beginning to end," but the world values only the first half of life — "does not value its second half of bodily decline." _Dust to Dust_ is not a book about death but about _this life_ and the unexpected path to living it to the full.

### The chiasm of life
Wilkin frames a human life as a chiasm: progressive development from first breath to middle age, then gradual decline until final breath. Before the fall, human beings aged without bodily decline; now we must suffer loss of function and independence. But we can grow strong in wisdom even as our bodies weaken.

> "As aging sets in, we tend to think only of what we will lose in our appearance. We forget to ask what might be gained in our souls." (Wilkin, p. 149)

### The gifts of older age
- Learning to number our days
- Gaining wisdom about what — and _who_ — matters most
- A call to age gracefully is a call to undergo suffering while maintaining hope in God's promises
- "Suffering and loss are skilled teachers. What do they teach? Humility. Patience. Perspective. Holiness. The fruit of the Spirit." (Wilkin, p. 99)

### Eldercare — "weighty" vs "burdensome"
> "There is a difference between a responsibility that is _weighty_ and one that is _burdensome_."

Speaking of eldercare as a burden wrongly connotes injustice or unforeseen calamity. The Christian alternative:
> "Godly wisdom views the elderly as people to love, not problems to solve. Worldly wisdom increasingly agrees that the elderly are problems before they are people." (Wilkin, p. 122)

### "Do what you wish"
Not catering to our own preferences, but treating our neighbors the way we would want to be treated at each life stage. Do what you wish would be done for you. This is the path to a life of consequence (Wilkin, p. 55).

### On honoring the wishes of aging parents
Wilkin draws on her experience caring for aging parents: while personal agency may decline, we should seek to honor their wishes whenever possible. When we don't take into consideration what they want, we confirm their fear that they are problems to solve.

> Do our speech and actions convey respect or thinly veiled frustration? Do we recognize our aging parents as fully human, fully adult, worthy of honor? (Wilkin, p. 129)

### The hope
For those united to Christ: while we "have a divine appointment with death," that day "is both an ending and a beginning" — an appointment with the end of our sin and the beginning of our sinlessness (Wilkin, pp. 162–63).

## Por que isso me interessa

The "people to love, not problems to solve" framing is the sort of thesis that lands hardest when read early — before the eldercare decisions actually arrive. Wilkin's chiasm frame (development → decline → wisdom) gives a vocabulary for the transition that resists both the world's denial of decline and the despair that decline inevitably means diminishment.
