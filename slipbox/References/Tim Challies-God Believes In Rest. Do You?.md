# God Believes In Rest. Do You?

A short, pastoral argument for taking rest seriously without having to "sanctify" it. Challies observes that Christians often feel the need to *justify* their rest — as if rest has to be baptized in "holy" activity to count.

## Core argument

- God rested on the seventh day, not from exhaustion but to **weave a pattern into the fabric of the world**: rhythm of labor that gives way to rest.
- The Decalogue makes this rhythm a *law*: "Six days you shall labor… but the seventh day is a Sabbath to the Lord."
- The new-covenant shape of rest: not primarily a day, but a **person** — not an occasion, but a Savior. Rest = dependence + eschatology (we rest in him now and ultimately).
- God also gives rest for **practical, physical reasons** — we are weak, finite, and need it. We can do more good, bring more blessing, serve his cause better *if* we rest.
- "There is a world of difference between rest and laziness, or between refreshment and idleness." One person sitting at the lake is rested; the same person taught VBS, rested. One person's nap is another person's burnout.

## The wrong reflex

Christians often:
- Sanctify rest by adding "holy" activities to it (VBS, ministry, service projects)
- Treat the most reverent Sunday afternoon as the one that leaves you worn down
- Feel guilty enjoying a gift they've been given (cf. "children who receive a toy for Christmas, then feel guilty when they play with it")

Challies' rebuke: **we deny ourselves a good gift of God if we refuse to rest in the ways that are truly restful to us.** The freedom to rest is itself part of the Christian life. Different temperaments, circumstances, and seasons call for different forms of rest.

## Closing exhortation

> "Rest to admit that the world is not yours to save, but God's. Rest to remember that you are not divine, but human. Rest to establish in your own mind and others', that you are not infinite, but finite, and that God made you for rest in just the same way he made you for work. Both are his idea. Both are his command. Both are necessary to serve his purposes."
