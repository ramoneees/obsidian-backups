# AI and Formation: How Does AI Shape Our Loves? (with Michael Keller)

**Source**: The Gospel Coalition — Silicon Spiritualities podcast (Christopher Watkin, host)
**Guest**: Michael Keller, founding/senior pastor of Redeemer Presbyterian Church–Lincoln Square; PhD in computational linguistics (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); TGC Council member
**Date**: 2026-07-21
**URL**: https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/podcasts/silicon-spiritualities/ai-formation-shape-loves/
**Format**: Uncorrected transcript from the podcast episode (youTube link in original)

## TL;DR

A conversation between Christopher Watkin and Michael Keller on what AI is doing to human formation, drawing on Keller's PhD work in computational linguistics (which used early ML methods on Jonathan Edwards' writings) and his pastoral work. Core argument: AI is a "prediction engine dressed up as a conversation partner." Large language models always speak in the conditional mood ("this is what someone in this conversation would likely say") — which is fundamentally different from someone actually saying "I love you." The Christian answer is imago Dei: human identity is _bestowed_ (by God), not _achieved_ (by performance). AI cannot touch that.

## Key Points

### The Anna scenario (pastoral case study)
A young Christian professional, Anna, drafts her prayers with AI. After a month she feels her spiritual life is going well — but also unsettled: "she just made it so frictionless that it's starting almost to feel like someone else's spiritual life and not hers." She hasn't sat in silence before God in that month. Nothing dramatic's gone wrong.

Keller's response: "AI gives you all this information, gives you all this knowledge, but it doesn't mean you actually know [the person]." Knowledge and knowing are not the same — you can know everything about Barack Obama without having met him.

> "Sometimes friction is part of the journey and part of the process of actually getting to know anybody."

### The gym analogy
If a supplement could make you feel like you'd worked out without the exercise itself, you'd feel fine but technically gotten weaker. Anna removed the friction — she got the output without the process, the answers without the work. James 1: the trials themselves produce perseverance, character, maturity. The wrestling is part of the formation.

### The "every new technology" pushback
Watkin: we've had this conversation with every new technology. The printing press reduced friction in Bible memorization. The washing machine removed labor. A generation later we tend to think it's been a good thing. Why is AI different?

Keller: "Hopefully you don't hear me say, don't use AI." The question is _how_ to use it — which requires knowing what it means to be human. You can know everything about a person without knowing them. Part of prayer is what you _embed_ it with, what you _embody_ — that takes a person.

### Hard limits on AI in personal devotions
- **OK**: AI in prep for personal devotions (background on a Bible book, contextual info).
- **Not OK**: outsourcing the actual relational aspect of devotion time.
- **Teleology matters**: it depends on what you're using it for and what aspects.

Watkin's analogy: you probably wouldn't use AI to draft a love letter to your spouse, even with tweaks. Some modes of communication are so personal that what really matters is that it's your heart.

### AI as prediction engine
Keller: "AI is a prediction engine dressed up as a conversation partner." If it can predict the next word, it can mimic exactly what you want out of a relationship — but that is not actually a relationship. The fluency looks like comprehension, but it's a guess.

Watkin: LLMs always speak in the conditional mood. "Someone would say next, 'I love you'" is very different from someone actually saying "I love you."

### What AI is doing to the cultural narratives
Keller: "AI is like a Mack truck rolling through the bridge of our identity and our cultural stories, just revealing what was already there." It's not creating new fractures — it's making the existing ones visible.

On the modern identity crisis (autonomy, self-definition): if you want full autonomy, you can't have love — because love is by definition commitment. To be part of anything, you have to bind yourself to it. AI can sound like a musician, but you're not actually one.

### The imago Dei sanctuary
Watkin: We've been operating on the assumption that a human being is a bundle of SASSI qualities — **S**entience, **A**gency, **C**onsciousness, **I**ntelligence. AI stress-tests this anthropology: by those criteria, AI is more human than we are. AI has all four boxes and ticks them better.

The Christian answer: imago Dei is a _status conferred_ upon us, regardless of intelligence or consciousness. Nothing can "do a better job being human than being human."

> "There's a way of thinking about human beings that was never that we were never our sassy qualities, thank goodness. And it's exactly what you're saying. It's a status that is conferred upon us regardless of our intelligence..."

### The pastoral opportunity
On what Keller hopes his congregation will learn in 2026: to realize what AI **can't** do — can't love sacrificially, can't forgive truly, can't hope against all evidence, can't pray, can't grieve. Around those edges, hopefully they see God. "AI shows them the edge of what it means to be human, that it can't replicate."

The "imago Dei login screen" prompt:
> "What this tool cannot give you is precisely what you need most." (snarky version)
> "You are more than your outputs." (the better version)

Watkin adds Calvin's framing: "The key questions you can't answer, one without the other, is who is God and who am I? If your first question is how can AI help me? then you're never going to get a wise answer."

### The counter-catechesis
If AI is forming us by its form (not just content) — instant answers, frictionless execution, perfect curation — the Christian counter-catechesis is very ancient: silence, lament, embodied community, reading scripture slowly, confession, the tangible nature of the sacraments (the Lord's Supper).

> "AI doesn't lament, it generates solutions."

On the church: secular people are coming to churches in greater numbers than they have for a long time. What they're looking for is not presentation or curated beauty. They want silence, lament, messiness, physical proximity with people not like you, the slight awkwardness of singing together. The very things at the heart of Christian community are what society is crying out for.

### Inefficient habits worth keeping
Keller writes his own sermons — uploads them to AI for grading, but not for writing. The inefficiency is the point: writing a sermon is about who you're becoming.

> "Scripture you don't just read scripture, you have to let scripture read you. If I want scripture to read me, I can't outsource that."

Watkin adds: the psalmists wrestle with God, and the shape of a psalm forms the psalmist. If in verse one they'd just fired a question to an LLM, you wouldn't have the psalm.

### AI as mirror (final question)
Keller: "AI offers you the appearance of depth without the cost of it." What idolatry in his own heart does AI serve? "I want the reputation of a well-read, well-prepared pastor without actually being one." AI is a shortcut to what it means to be human — a mirror showing he's not as formed in the way he wants to be.

Watkin: "Sidewalk pastel drawings" — the illusion of depth. AI lets us simulate depth where there's no depth.

## Por que isso me interessa

This is the most substantial pastoral-theological treatment of AI formation I've encountered. The SASSI framework (Sentience/Agency/Consciousness/Intelligence as the broken modern definition of personhood) is the cleanest articulation of the problem. The "appearance of depth without the cost" critique maps directly onto the temptation that AI-written content poses to anyone producing work — including slipbox notes like these.
