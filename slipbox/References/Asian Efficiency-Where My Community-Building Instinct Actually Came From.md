# Where My Community-Building Instinct Actually Came From

**Source**: [Asian Efficiency](https://www.asianefficiency.com/mindsets/where-my-community-instinct-came-from/)
**Author**: Thanh Pham
**Published**: 2026-07-26 (last updated 2026-07-06)
**Category**: Mindsets

## TL;DR

Thanh traces his dinner-party-hosting habit (50+ parties in Austin over two years) back to his mother, a Vietnamese refugee who arrived in the Netherlands with a suitcase and immediately began gathering other refugees for meals. The community instinct wasn't something he figured out — it was something he absorbed by watching it modeled as a child. Values transmit through demonstration, not instruction.

## The Story

### The Suitcase and the Dinner Table

Thanh's parents are Vietnamese refugees who met at a refugee camp in the Philippines, then resettled in the Netherlands with nothing — a suitcase, some documents, the clothes they had. They knew no one and didn't speak the language. His mother's **first instinct was to start hosting** — finding other Vietnamese refugees and gathering them for dinners, karaoke nights, small gatherings in the middle of uncertainty.

She wasn't stable herself when she started. She was still building. But the first thing she reached for wasn't security — it was connection. For other people.

### The Realization That Took Thirty Years

When a podcast host asked "where does your community-building instinct come from?", Thanh had to trace it back. He thought he'd figured out the dinner-party pattern himself — as a response to loneliness after moving to Austin in 2014, a choice, an experiment that worked. He had achieved most of his professional goals but had "a lot of acquaintances and very few actual friendships." The book *30 Lessons for Living* (wisdom from people at the end of their lives) drove home: **the quality of your relationships determines the quality of your life. Not your achievements. Not your career.**

But when he actually traced the pattern back, he realized he hadn't figured it out at all. He had just *remembered* it. From a childhood spent watching his mother gather people together when she had almost nothing, because gathering people was what you did.

### What Gets Passed Down Without Words

Thanh's mother never sat him down and explained why community matters. She never gave a framework or philosophy. She just did it — consistently, quietly, in the middle of circumstances that would have given her every excuse not to. And he absorbed it without knowing he was absorbing it.

> "This is how a lot of values actually get transmitted. Not through explicit instruction but through modeling. The people around us when we're young demonstrate a way of being in the world, and we internalize it so naturally that we mistake it for our own idea later."

### Generosity as Habit

Thanh pushes back on the "I'll start when I'm more settled" approach to community building. His mother didn't wait — she was in the middle of settling and still reached out. The people who build great communities aren't doing it from a position of abundance; they're doing it because they made a decision about what kind of person they want to be, and then they keep doing it regardless of circumstances.

## The Underlying Argument

This is a piece about **how values actually propagate** — not through teaching, not through frameworks, but through repeated demonstration in a child's environment. The implication is that the same transmission works in reverse: you model the values you want to pass on, not by explaining them but by living them visibly enough that they get absorbed.

It's also a piece about **timing for generosity** — conditions for generosity are never going to be perfect, and waiting for perfect is just a way of never starting. The decision precedes the circumstances, not the other way around.
